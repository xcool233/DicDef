WORD: Gormless
PRONUNCIATION: ˈɡɔːmləs

DEFINITION: Lacking intelligence, sense, or initiative; foolish and dim-witted
PART_OF_SPEECH: Adjective
USAGE: The gormless clerk stared blankly at the computer for ten minutes before realising it wasn't plugged in.

DEFINITION: Lacking awareness or alertness; vacant and clueless in manner or expression
PART_OF_SPEECH: Adjective
USAGE: She gave a gormless shrug when asked why she had forgotten the meeting for the third week running.

SYNONYMS: dim-witted, dopey, vacant, witless, clueless

ETYMOLOGY: From dialectal English "gaum," meaning understanding, heed, or attention — itself derived from Old Norse "gaumr," meaning care or heed. The suffix "-less" comes from Old English "-lēas," meaning without. Together, "gormless" literally means "without gaum" — that is, without sense or awareness.

ADDITIONAL_ETYMOLOGY: The word's journey begins in Old Norse, where "gaumr" carried the sense of attention, notice, and careful regard. This term passed into Northern and Midland dialects of Middle English as "gaum" (also spelled "gorm"), where it retained the meaning of understanding or heed — as in "to take gaum of" something, meaning to pay it notice.

The positive form "gaum" or "gorm" was once in common dialectal use across the north of England and Scotland, but gradually fell out of standard usage while its negation — "gormless" — survived and eventually spread into broader British English during the 19th century.

The "-less" suffix attaches to Old English "-lēas," a highly productive Germanic suffix meaning "devoid of" or "free from," seen also in words like "reckless," "feckless," and "hapless." Interestingly, "feckless" followed a strikingly similar path: "feck" (from "effect") was once a living dialectal word in Scots English before fading, leaving only its negative form behind.

"Gormless" is recorded in print from the mid-19th century and remains chiefly British in usage, carrying a tone that is more affectionately exasperated than truly cruel — the gormless person is less malicious than simply, hopelessly oblivious.
