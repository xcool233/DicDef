WORD: Plight
PRONUNCIATION: plaɪt

DEFINITION: A dangerous, difficult, or unfortunate situation, especially one from which it is hard to escape
PART_OF_SPEECH: Noun
USAGE: The plight of the stranded hikers worsened as the storm closed in.

DEFINITION: To pledge or promise solemnly, especially in the phrase <i>plight one's troth</i> (to promise fidelity or marriage)
PART_OF_SPEECH: Verb
USAGE: They plighted their troth beneath the old oak tree at dawn.

SYNONYMS: predicament, quandary, dilemma, straits, difficulty

WORD_FORMS: plighted (adj.), plighting (v.), plighter (n.)

ETYMOLOGY: The noun meaning "predicament" comes from Anglo-French <i>plit</i>, a variant of Old French <i>pleit</i> ("a fold, a condition"), from Latin <i>plicare</i>, "to fold". The verb meaning "to pledge" is a separate word, from Old English <i>plihtan</i>, "to endanger, to pledge".

ADDITIONAL_ETYMOLOGY: In Middle English, the noun appeared as <i>plit</i> or <i>plyt</i> and meant "a condition, state, or manner of being", with no suggestion of misfortune. It grew from the Old French sense of a "fold", which came to mean the way something is arranged or "folded", and so its state or condition. The Latin source <i>plicare</i> is also the root of English <i>pliant</i>, <i>complicate</i>, <i>display</i>, <i>employ</i> and <i>pleat</i>.

The modern spelling with <i>-gh-</i> arose in the 16th century through confusion with the unrelated verb <i>plight</i>, "to pledge". That verb descends from Old English <i>pliht</i>, "danger, risk, peril", and is related to German <i>Pflicht</i>, "duty". Over time the spellings merged, and the noun's meaning drifted from a neutral "condition" toward the negative sense of a bad state, so that today we speak of "a sorry plight".

The two words now look identical, but one traces back to folding and the other to risk and obligation. Only the phrase <i>plight one's troth</i> keeps the older pledging sense in everyday use.
