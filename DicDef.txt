WORD: Sinister
PRONUNCIATION: ˈsɪnɪstər

PALETTE_HARMONY: analogous
PALETTE_PRIMARY: #8b1a1a

DEFINITION: Giving the impression that something harmful, evil, or threatening is about to happen; suggestive of malevolent intent
PART_OF_SPEECH: Adjective
USAGE: A sinister silence settled over the house as the lights flickered and went out one by one.

DEFINITION: Pertaining to, or situated on, the left side; in heraldry, the side of a shield to the bearer's left (the viewer's right)
PART_OF_SPEECH: Adjective
USAGE: The coat of arms showed a lion on the sinister side of the shield, facing away from the centre.

DEFINITION: Foreboding misfortune; regarded as ill-omened or unlucky
PART_OF_SPEECH: Adjective
USAGE: The villagers saw the crows gathering on the rooftops as a sinister sign of the hard winter ahead.

SYNONYMS: menacing, ominous, malevolent, threatening, baleful

WORD_FORMS: sinisterly (adv.), sinisterness (n.), sinistral (adj.), sinistrous (adj.)

ETYMOLOGY: From Old French <i>sinistre</i>, from Latin <i>sinister</i>, meaning "left" or "on the left side," and by extension "unlucky" or "inauspicious." The ultimate origin of the Latin word is uncertain.

ADDITIONAL_ETYMOLOGY: The Latin <i>sinister</i> originally meant simply "left," as opposed to <i>dexter</i>, "right." Its sense of misfortune arose from the way Roman augurs interpreted omens. Roman priests, facing south, took signs from the east (their left) as favourable, so <i>sinister</i> could mean "auspicious" in Latin. Greek augural practice, however, treated the left as the unlucky side, and this view, combined with the widespread cultural prejudice against left-handedness, eventually dominated. The Greek word <noto><i>ἀριστερός</i></noto> (aristeros), "left," was itself a euphemism, literally "the better one," used to avoid naming the unlucky side directly.

The word entered Middle English in the 15th century through Old French, carrying both the literal sense of "left" and the figurative senses of "unfavourable" and "unlucky." By the 16th century the sense of "evil" or "malevolent" had taken firm hold, as in the idea of a sinister motive or design. The literal sense survives in heraldry, where the sinister side of a shield is the bearer's left. The related phrase <i>bend sinister</i> is a diagonal band running from the shield's upper right to lower left, which was popularly (though inaccurately) associated with illegitimacy.

The same root gave Italian <i>sinistra</i> and Spanish <i>siniestro</i>, both of which retain the sense of "left" alongside "ominous." English <i>sinistral</i>, meaning "left-handed" or "coiling to the left" (as in certain snail shells), is a modern scientific borrowing from the same Latin source.
